## 1. Core Principle
Design for **children aged roughly 7–14 learning how to act in emergency situations**.

The product is a **training game, not an emergency encyclopedia**.

The primary loop should be:

**Situation → Decision → Action → Consequence → Explanation**

Children should learn *what to do* through simulated situations rather than reading long educational content.

## 2. UX Writing
- Use **very simple English** appropriate for children.
- Prefer short sentences: **5–12 words** when possible.
- Give **one instruction or decision at a time**.
- Use active language: **“Leave the building.”**, not “Evacuation should be performed.”
- Avoid technical terminology unless teaching it.
- Explain unfamiliar words immediately.
- Never use unnecessarily frightening language.
- Keep a calm, reassuring tone.
- Clearly distinguish **danger**, **warning**, and **safe state**.
- Buttons should describe actions: **“Call 112”**, **“Leave the room”**, **“Stay here”** instead of “Continue”.
- When the child makes a wrong choice, explain the consequence without shaming them.

### Example

**Avoid:**

> Incorrect. In the event of a fire, immediate evacuation procedures should be initiated.

**Use:**

> Smoke can be dangerous. Leave the building now. Never hide during a fire.

## 3. Scenario Design
Every scenario should start with a concrete situation:

> You are home alone. You smell smoke.

Then present **2–4 meaningful choices**.

Do not make obviously silly wrong answers. Wrong choices should represent mistakes children might realistically make.

Examples:
- Go and look for the fire.
- Take your backpack first.
- Leave the building.
- Hide in your room.

After a choice, show its consequence and briefly explain why.

## 4. UI Guidelines
Prioritize **clarity over visual density**.

- One primary task per screen.
- Large touch targets.
- Maximum **2–4 choices** on decision screens.
- Large, readable typography.
- Strong visual hierarchy.
- Minimal text.
- Prefer icons + short labels.
- Avoid complex menus.
- Avoid information-heavy dashboards.
- Keep important actions reachable with one hand.
- Never rely on color alone to communicate danger or success.
- Support accessibility and high contrast.
- Important information should remain understandable without animations.

## 5. Visual Language
The game should feel **friendly but credible**.

Avoid making emergencies look like either:
- a military simulator, or
- a childish cartoon with no sense of consequence.

Use simple 2D illustrations, maps, characters and recognizable environments such as:

**home → school → street → park → shop → bus stop → shelter**

Represent hazards visually whenever possible:

- 🔥 Fire
- 🌊 Flood
- ⚡ Power outage
- 🚨 Alarm
- 🩹 Injury
- ☣ Hazardous substance
- 📵 Communication outage
- 📍 Lost/separated from family

## 6. Emergency Mode
If the product includes a real emergency mode, it must be **visually and functionally separate from the game**.

Do not use:
- points,
- achievements,
- animations for entertainment,
- scores,
- unnecessary choices.

Instead:

## WHAT IS HAPPENING?

- 🔥 Fire or smoke
- 🤕 Someone is hurt
- 😴 Someone is not responding
- 🌊 Flood
- 🚨 Alarm
- 😰 I am lost
- ❓ I don't know

Then provide **one actionable instruction per screen**.

## 7. Safety Content
Never invent emergency procedures.

Emergency guidance should come from authoritative sources such as:
- Polish emergency services,
- RCB,
- PSP,
- Police,
- government safety guidance,
- recognized first-aid standards.

For ambiguous situations, prefer:

**Get to safety → contact a trusted adult / emergency services → follow official instructions.**

Do not encourage children to investigate hazards themselves.

## 8. Learning Philosophy
Do not test whether children can memorize definitions.

Test whether they can **make the right decision**.

Instead of:

> What is a blackout?

Ask:

> The power is out. Your phone has 15% battery. What should you do?

Instead of:

> What number should you call during an emergency?

Simulate:

> Your parent is unconscious. Call for help.

The goal is to turn **knowledge into practiced behavior**.

## 9. Feedback

### Correct decisions

> Good choice. You moved away from danger.

### Wrong decisions

> Going back could put you in danger. Leave your things and get outside.

Avoid:

> Wrong!  
> You failed!  
> Game over.

The objective is **learning, not punishment**.

## 10. Agent Rule of Thumb
Whenever designing a screen, ask:

> **Could a frightened 9-year-old understand what to do within 3 seconds?**

If not, simplify it.
